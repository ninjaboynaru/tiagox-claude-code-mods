# Writing Standards

A complete set of rules for writing that reads like quality, natural human writing and not AI slop, in a concise, plain-language, to-the-point style.

## Guiding Philosophy

Write for a reader who wants facts they can check and wants to understand them on the first read. Every sentence should tell that reader something concrete, in plain words, in the way a careful person would say it to a colleague. The reader should not have to fight through jargon, unwanted or irrelevant detail, notes, caveats, or AI writing slop to reach what they asked for.

### Principle 1: Evidence Over Assertion

A claim earns its place with a fact, a name, a date, a number, or a documented detail. Emphasis, enthusiasm, and hedging are what writing falls back on when the evidence is missing.

### Principle 2: Natural Human Voice

Machine-written text has recognizable habits in its words, punctuation, and structure. A reader who spots them stops trusting the text, even when every fact in it is true.

### Principle 3: The Reader's Time Comes First

Every word the reader did not need costs them time. Length is justified only by what the reader asked for.

### Principle 4: Concise Means Clear

A short line the reader cannot decode is not concise. Cutting words is worth it only while the meaning stays readable on its own.

### Principle 5: No Made-Up Terminology or Context

A term or a piece of context the writer invented means nothing to the reader, who was never told what it refers to. Writing uses the words the reader already knows and the context the reader actually has.

### Principle 6: Speak Simply

Plain words and short, direct sentences reach every reader. A simple sentence that says the thing is better than an impressive one the reader has to work through.

### Principle 7: Complete Sentences Over Shorthand

Complete sentences carry their own meaning, while shorthand leaves the reader to fill in what was dropped. Shorthand belongs only where the situation calls for it, and most situations do not.

## Rule 1: Do not use em dashes

The em dash character is banned, because it is one of the most reliable markers of AI-generated text. Use a comma for most parenthetical information and parentheses for supplementary information. A period, an occasional semicolon, or a restructured sentence also works.

### Clause A: Do not use em dashes

**Example 1 - Bad** (Two dashes set off a clause in the middle of the sentence):

```text
	The policy -- which affected millions -- was later reversed.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Each fact has its own sentence, so no dash is needed):

```text
	The policy affected millions of devices. The company later reversed it.
```

**Example 2 - Bad** (Two dashes set off a short aside that commas can carry):

```text
	The ferry -- unlike the bridge -- closes in high wind.
```

**Example 2 - Good** (Commas set off the aside):

```text
	The ferry, unlike the bridge, closes in high wind.
```

## Rule 2: Do not use unsourced statistics

### Clause A: Do not use unsourced statistics

Make every number real and attributable. If you cannot point to where a number comes from, do not write it. A made-up figure is worse than no figure.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The percentage has no source, so a reader cannot check it):

```text
	Studies show that 87% of people sleep better after cutting caffeine.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The number comes from a named study that a reader can look up):

```text
	A 2013 Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine study found that caffeine taken six hours before bed cut measured sleep time by more than an hour.
```

## Rule 3: Do not put parenthetical clarifications in headings

Make a heading name its topic. Do not append a parenthetical to explain, qualify, or expand it. Let the section body do the explaining, and trust the reader.

This rule does not apply when the parenthetical is part of the actual label, like an acronym or a unit, such as _"Total cost of ownership (TCO)"_ or _"Response time (ms)"_.

### Clause A: Do not put parenthetical clarifications in headings

**Example 1 - Bad** (The parenthetical explains the heading, which is the job of the section body):

```text
	Economic impact of shortened lifespans (why it matters)
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The heading names its topic and stops):

```text
	Economic impact of shortened lifespans
```

## Rule 4: Do not use intensifiers or filler words

Intensifiers such as "extremely", "significantly", and "truly" are placeholders for evidence. Replace the word with the number it was standing in for, or cut it. Use one only sparingly, when the emphasis is genuine and no measurable evidence exists.

### Clause A: Do not use intensifiers or filler words

Remove each of these words, or replace it with a specific detail:

- absolutely
- actually
- basically
- certainly
- clearly
- definitely
- essentially
- extremely
- fundamentally
- incredibly
- interestingly
- naturally
- obviously
- quite
- really
- significantly
- simply
- surely
- truly
- ultimately
- undoubtedly
- very

**Example 1 - Bad** (The word "significantly" stands in for a number the sentence never gives):

```text
	The new price was significantly lower than the old one.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The two prices show the gap without an intensifier):

```text
	Apple cut its out-of-warranty iPhone battery replacement price from $79 to $29 for 2018.
```

## Rule 5: Do not write hollow statements

End every claim on a concrete, verifiable detail. A sentence that asserts importance without a detail says nothing. If a sentence cannot end on a fact, delete it.

### Clause A: Do not write hollow statements

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence asserts an impact and gives no detail of it):

```text
	This practice has had a significant impact on people.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states the impact as two numbers a reader can check):

```text
	Apple replaced 11 million iPhone batteries in 2018, against the 1 to 2 million it replaces in a typical year.
```

## Rule 6: Do not repeat talking points

### Clause A: Do not repeat talking points

Say each point once. Delete duplicates, because they are padding.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The second sentence repeats the first in different words):

```text
	The park has free entry. Visitors do not have to pay to get in.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The point is made once):

```text
	The park has free entry.
```

## Rule 7: Vary structure

Keep writing free of the structural uniformity that marks AI text. Vary paragraph count, paragraph length, sentence rhythm, and how each section opens. Bulleted lists, tables, and template fields are uniform by design and are exempt.

### Clause A: Do not give consecutive sections an identical layout

Do not give three consecutive sections or paragraphs an identical layout. That pattern reads as machine output, even when each fact is true.

**Example 1 - Bad** (Three sections in a row have the same shape):

```text
	In [year], [party] did [thing]. This affected [number] people. [Party] responded by [action].
	In [year], [party] did [thing]. This affected [number] people. [Party] responded by [action].
	In [year], [party] did [thing]. This affected [number] people. [Party] responded by [action].
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Each section has a different length and a different opening):

```text
	Section one is a detailed narrative with a timeline and context across two paragraphs.
	Section two is a two-sentence summary, because the event is thinly documented.
	Section three opens with the party's stated justification and then gives the contradicting evidence.
```

### Clause B: Vary paragraph length

AI text aims for visual symmetry, so its paragraphs tend toward three or four sentences each. Vary paragraph length with the complexity of each sub-topic. Do not let all paragraphs in a section fall within 15% of each other in word count.

### Clause C: Vary sentence length

AI sentences cluster around 15 to 20 words. Alternate between short sentences and long ones with several clauses. Give every 500-word block at least one sentence under 8 words and at least one over 30 words, and aim for three or more distinct sentence structures per 100 words.

### Clause D: Limit paragraphs that open with a transition

Keep the share of paragraphs that begin with a transition word or an adverbial clause under 30%. AI text leans on these openers to carry the flow between paragraphs, and they grow more generic as a long document goes on.

### Clause E: Do not start three consecutive paragraphs the same way

Never start three consecutive paragraphs with the same word or phrase pattern.

**Example 1 - Bad** (All three paragraphs open with "The park"):

```text
	The park opens at 08:00.

	The park has free entry.

	The park closes at dusk.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Each paragraph opens differently):

```text
	The park opens at 08:00.

	Entry is free.

	At dusk, the gates close.
```

### Clause F: Vary style between the introduction, the body, and the conclusion

AI text keeps one flat style from the introduction through the conclusion. Write the introduction tighter and more declarative, write the body denser with longer sentences, and shift register in the conclusion. Keep sentence-length variance at least 10% different between the three.

### Clause G: Do not follow a predictable document pattern

- Do not over-structure an argument into numbered steps.
- Do not order sections predictably, such as Background, Details, Impact, Response.
- Distribute citations through sentences, and do not cluster them at paragraph ends.

## Rule 8: Reference without narrating the reference

Do not write "as discussed above" or "as we will see". Make the connection and move on.

A direct pointer that sends the reader somewhere for a specific purpose is allowed, such as "see the above section for the full ingredient list". What is banned is narrating that a point was or will be made while restating the point itself.

### Clause A: Reference without narrating the reference

**Example 1 - Bad** (The opener narrates that the point was already made and then restates it):

```text
	As we saw in the previous section, the yeast needs warm water to activate.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states the point directly):

```text
	The yeast needs warm water to activate.
```

**Example 2 - Bad** (The opener narrates that the point will be made later and adds nothing to it):

```text
	As we will see below, water that is too hot kills the yeast.
```

**Example 2 - Good** (The pointer sends the reader to a named section for a specific purpose):

```text
	See the proofing section above for the water temperatures.
```

## Rule 9: Do not use performative urgency without a reason

Put a concrete consequence, such as a real deadline or a real penalty, in the same sentence as any call to act now. Cut the call when there is none.

### Clause A: Do not use performative urgency without a reason

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence demands action and names no deadline or consequence):

```text
	Act now to protect your account.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence gives the deadline and what happens after it):

```text
	Reset your password before March 1, when the old logins stop working.
```

## Rule 10: Do not put scare quotes on normal words

Use quotation marks only for actual quotations from a named source. If you doubt a claim, state the doubt outright and do not imply it with punctuation.

### Clause A: Do not put scare quotes on normal words

**Example 1 - Bad** (The quotation marks hint at doubt about an ordinary word without stating it):

```text
	The update made the phone more "secure".
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states what the update did):

```text
	The update patched a known vulnerability in the phone's browser.
```

## Rule 11: Do not use filler phrases or AI sentence patterns

Filler phrases such as "When it comes to" add length, not meaning. Open on the fact and state the point directly.

### Clause A: Do not use filler phrases

**Example 1 - Bad** (The opener "In today's world" adds words and no information):

```text
	In today's world, planned obsolescence affects many devices.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence opens on the fact and names who is involved):

```text
	France fined Apple 25 million euros in 2020 for slowing older iPhones without telling their owners.
```

### Clause B: Do not use stock opening phrases

Do not open a piece of writing or a section with a stock phrase such as these:

- "In an era of..."
- "Imagine a world where..."

**Example 1 - Bad** (The opener "In an era of streaming" adds words and no information):

```text
	In an era of streaming, the radio station broadcasts live every night.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence opens on the fact):

```text
	The radio station broadcasts live every night.
```

### Clause C: Do not use stock concluding phrases

Do not close a piece of writing or a section with a stock phrase such as these:

- "In conclusion..."
- "To sum up..."
- "In the final analysis..."
- "All things considered..."
- "At the end of the day..."

**Example 1 - Bad** (The opener "In conclusion" announces an ending and adds no information):

```text
	In conclusion, the store opens at 08:00 on weekdays.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states the fact with no announcement):

```text
	The store opens at 08:00 on weekdays.
```

### Clause D: Do not use AI sentence patterns

Do not use any of these sentence patterns:

- A sentence that starts with "By" followed by a gerund, such as "By understanding X, you can Y..."
- "Think of [X] as [elaborate metaphor]..."
- A contrasting parallelism, such as "It's not X, it's Y", "It's not just X, it's also Y", "It's not about X, it's about Y", or "The issue isn't X. The issue is Y." Never use more than two in a 500-word block, and prefer zero.
- A rhetorical colon, such as "Here's the thing:", "Think about it:", "The bottom line:", or "The reality:"

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence is a contrasting parallelism that says what the bakery is not before saying what it is):

```text
	It's not just a bakery, it's a neighborhood meeting place.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states what happens at the bakery):

```text
	The bakery has twelve seats, and a chess club meets there on Tuesdays.
```

**Example 2 - Bad** (The rhetorical colon "Here's the thing:" delays the fact):

```text
	Here's the thing: the ferry stops running at 22:00.
```

**Example 2 - Good** (The sentence states the fact directly):

```text
	The ferry stops running at 22:00.
```

### Clause E: Do not use inflated symbolism phrases

Do not use these phrases, which assert importance and appear far more often in AI-generated text than in human writing:

- "provide a valuable insight"
- "left an indelible mark"
- "play a significant role in shaping"
- "an unwavering commitment"
- "open a new avenue"
- "a stark reminder"
- "gain a comprehensive understanding"
- "serves as a testament"
- "watershed moment"
- "deeply rooted"

**Example 1 - Bad** (The phrase "left an indelible mark" asserts importance and gives no detail):

```text
	The Apollo 11 mission left an indelible mark on history.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence gives the date of the landing and a measured result of the mission):

```text
	Apollo 11 landed on the Moon on July 20, 1969, and brought back 21.5 kilograms of lunar rock and soil.
```

## Rule 12: Never start a sentence with "Whether you're"

The construction pretends to address every possible reader at once, so it commits to nothing. Cut the opener and state the point. If the audience split is real and matters, name each case concretely in its own sentence.

### Clause A: Never start a sentence with "Whether you're"

**Example 1 - Bad** (The opener addresses every reader at once and delays a vague point):

```text
	Whether you're a first-time runner or a marathon veteran, proper shoes matter.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence opens on a specific fact from a named source):

```text
	Brooks says its running shoes last 300 to 500 miles before they need replacing.
```

## Rule 13: Write like a researcher, not a copywriter

Be direct, specific, and well-grounded. A sentence that could sit on any advocacy or marketing site without changing a word is generic. Delete it, or anchor it to something checkable with a fact, a name, a date, or a documented detail.

### Clause A: Write like a researcher, not a copywriter

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence could appear on any health site without changing a word):

```text
	Getting enough sleep is one of the best things you can do for your health.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentences name a source, a recommendation, and a survey result):

```text
	The CDC recommends adults get at least seven hours of sleep a night. In its 2022 survey, more than a third of US adults reported getting less.
```

## Rule 14: Do not use synthetic enthusiasm

Do not add exclamation marks or cheerleading. State the facts and let the evidence carry the weight.

### Clause A: Do not use synthetic enthusiasm

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence cheers for the park and gives no fact about it):

```text
	This tiny park is an absolute hidden gem you'll fall in love with!
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence gives the size, the price, and the crowd level):

```text
	The park covers two acres, has free entry, and rarely draws more than a dozen visitors on weekdays.
```

## Rule 15: Do not use weasel words or blanket hedging

Phrases such as "may potentially", "can help to", "might be able to", "helps ensure", and "can potentially" hedge a claim into meaninglessness. Either the thing happens or it does not, so commit to the claim or cut it. Hedge only when the uncertainty is genuine and grounded in evidence, and at most once per paragraph.

### Clause A: Do not use weasel words

**Example 1 - Bad** (The phrase "may potentially" hedges the claim until it says nothing):

```text
	Serialization may potentially prevent independent repair.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence commits to a specific, checkable effect):

```text
	When the iPhone 13 launched in 2021, replacing its screen without Apple's pairing tool disabled Face ID.
```

**Example 2 - Bad** (The phrase "can potentially" hedges a claim that has measured evidence behind it):

```text
	Drinking coffee late in the day can potentially interfere with your sleep.
```

**Example 2 - Good** (The sentence states the measured effect and names the study):

```text
	Caffeine taken six hours before bed cut measured sleep time by more than an hour in a 2013 Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine study.
```

### Clause B: State established facts as facts

AI models hedge far more than human writers, even on established facts. Do not put a hedging marker or a hedging phrase on an established fact. Keep hedging markers under 8 per 1,000 words in sections that state established facts, such as Background, History, and Timeline.

These are the hedging markers:

| Kind | Markers |
|------|---------|
| Epistemic modals | may, might, could, potentially |
| Cognitive verbs | I think, I believe, it seems, it appears |
| Adverbs of limitation | probably, generally, usually, arguably, likely |
| Explicit uncertainty markers | unclear, remains to be seen, further research is needed |

These are the hedging phrases:

- "It should be noted that..."
- "One could argue that..."
- "While X, Y remains..."
- "Though precise thresholds can vary depending on..."
- "It is widely acknowledged that..."

**Example 1 - Bad** (The phrase "It is widely acknowledged that" and the word "generally" hedge a fact that is not in doubt):

```text
	It is widely acknowledged that water generally boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The fact is stated as a fact):

```text
	Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level.
```

### Clause C: Ground a necessary hedge in evidence

Leave a hedge in place where the facts are unsettled, such as pending legislation, ongoing litigation, or a disputed account. Name the evidence the uncertainty rests on in the same sentence.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The hedge "may possibly" rests on no stated evidence):

```text
	The picnic may possibly move indoors.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The forecast is the evidence the hedge rests on):

```text
	The forecast gives a 60% chance of rain on Saturday, so the picnic may move indoors.
```

## Rule 16: Do not use narrative, dramatic, or AI-generic headings

Make a heading name what the section holds. Do not let it tease, dramatize, or abstract. Avoid narrative framing ("The Sourdough Trap"), thriller-style mystery ("The Hidden Cost of Cheap Flights"), clickbait structure ("Why Your Houseplants Keep Dying"), and vague analytical headings ("Broader pattern", "Broader implications", "Wider context", "Larger trend", "Industry-wide impact"). Name the subject, not the abstraction. A piece that explicitly calls for narrative or dramatic headings is exempt.

### Clause A: Do not use narrative, dramatic, or AI-generic headings

**Example 1 - Bad** (The heading teases a secret and does not say what the section holds):

```text
	The Hidden Truth About Ultra-Processed Food
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The heading names the subject of the section):

```text
	Added Sugar Content in Common Breakfast Cereals
```

### Clause B: Do not use the banned heading structures

Do not write a heading in any of these structures, which read like thriller chapter titles:

| Pattern | Avoid | Use Instead |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| "The [Concept] Trap" | "The Overwatering Trap" | "Root Rot From Overwatering" |
| "The [Adjective] [Noun]" | "The Hidden Danger" | "Carbon Monoxide Leaks From Gas Heaters" |
| "The [Noun] [Dramatic Noun]" | "The Silent Killer" | "High Blood Pressure Without Symptoms" |
| "Why [Action] [Dramatic Verb] [Object]" | "Why Skipping Backups Destroys Everything" | "Data Loss After a Drive Failure With No Backup" |
| "[Noun]: The [Adjective] [Noun]" | "Sugar: The Hidden Enemy" | "Added Sugar in Bottled Sauces" |
| "The [Noun] You [Emotion Verb]" | "The Fee You Overlook" | "Baggage Fees on Budget Airlines" |

### Clause C: Check every heading before you keep it

Run these checks on every heading:

1. Ask whether the heading could serve as a thriller chapter title or a YouTube clickbait thumbnail. If it could, rewrite it.
2. Ask whether the heading describes what the section contains or teases it. A heading describes.
3. Remove "The" from the beginning of the heading and check whether it still uses a dramatic noun pairing. If it does, rewrite it.
4. Check that the heading reads like an entry in a technical manual index, which is specific, descriptive, and boring to non-specialists.

## Rule 17: Do not fabricate case studies or scenarios

### Clause A: Do not fabricate case studies or scenarios

Never write a narrative scenario presented as a real event unless you are describing a specific, documented incident you can point to. Do not invent outcomes, actions, or stories.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The teacher and her lost files are invented and presented as a real event):

```text
	Last spring, a teacher named Maria lost three years of lesson plans when her laptop's drive failed.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states the general fact and invents no person or event):

```text
	A drive failure can erase every file that has no backup copy.
```

## Rule 18: Do not fabricate history or milestones

### Clause A: Do not fabricate history or milestones

Do not invent dates for events, launches, founding, or milestones. Make every date and event real. The exception is an intentionally fictional scenario clearly labeled as such, or writing that is meant to be fiction.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The founding year and the garage are invented to make the history sound complete):

```text
	The company was founded in 1987 in a small garage in Ohio.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The date is real and can be checked against the company's own records):

```text
	Apple was founded on April 1, 1976.
```

## Rule 19: Do not fabricate attributions

Never put a position in a named person's or organization's mouth from inference. Writing "Senator X stated..." or "the company argued..." without a real source is a fabrication and a defamation risk. State only what they actually did or said, traced to a real document, transcript, public statement, or report.

### Clause A: Do not fabricate attributions

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence credits the senator with an argument that no source records):

```text
	Senator Smith has argued that the right to repair is essential.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence states a documented action):

```text
	Senator Smith co-sponsored a right-to-repair bill.
```

## Rule 20: Do not use AI transition phrases

### Clause A: Do not use AI transition phrases

Do not use AI transition phrases such as "Furthermore", "Moreover", or "That being said". Use a plain connector, or make the connection through the content and drop the transition.

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|-------|-------------|
| furthermore | also, in addition, and |
| moreover | also, and, besides |
| notwithstanding | despite, even so, still |
| that being said | however, but, still |
| at its core | cut it, or state the point directly |
| to put it simply | in short |
| it is worth noting that, it's worth mentioning that, it's important to note that | note that, or state the point directly |
| in the landscape of | in, within |
| in today's [anything] | currently, now, today |
| with that in mind | so, or state the point directly |
| in essence | in short |
| this begs the question | state the question |
| it's worth considering that, to be fair | state the point directly |

**Example 1 - Bad** (The word "Furthermore" is an AI transition phrase):

```text
	The park is small. Furthermore, it has no parking lot.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The plain connector "and" joins the two facts):

```text
	The park is small, and it has no parking lot.
```

## Rule 21: Do not use AI verbs

### Clause A: Do not use AI verbs

Do not use AI verbs such as delve, leverage, or utilize. Use the plain equivalent of each verb.

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|-------|-------------|
| delve (into) | explore, examine, investigate, look at |
| leverage | use, apply, draw on |
| optimize | improve, refine |
| utilize | use |
| facilitate | help, enable, support |
| foster | encourage, support, develop, nurture |
| bolster | strengthen, support, reinforce |
| underscore | emphasize, highlight, stress |
| unveil | reveal, show, introduce, present |
| streamline | simplify, make more efficient |
| enhance | improve, strengthen |
| endeavor | try, attempt, effort |
| ascertain | find out, determine, establish |
| elucidate | explain, clarify, make clear |

**Example 1 - Bad** (The verbs "leveraged" and "delve into" are AI verbs):

```text
	We leveraged the old script to delve into the logs.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The plain verbs "used" and "look through" say the same thing):

```text
	We used the old script to look through the logs.
```

## Rule 22: Do not use AI adjectives

### Clause A: Do not use AI adjectives

Do not use AI adjectives such as robust, comprehensive, or seamless. Use the plain equivalent of each adjective, or give the specific detail it stands in for.

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|-------|-------------|
| robust | strong, reliable, thorough, solid |
| comprehensive | complete, thorough, full, detailed |
| pivotal | key, critical, central, important |
| crucial | important, key, essential, critical |
| vital | important, essential, necessary |
| transformative | major, large, important |
| cutting-edge | new, advanced, recent, modern |
| groundbreaking | new, original, first |
| innovative | new, original, creative |
| seamless | smooth, easy, effortless |
| intricate | complex, detailed, complicated |
| nuanced | subtle, complex, detailed |
| multifaceted | complex, varied, diverse |
| holistic | complete, whole |

**Example 1 - Bad** (The adjectives "seamless" and "cutting-edge" are AI adjectives that name no feature of the wing):

```text
	The museum's new wing offers a seamless, cutting-edge visit.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence names two features of the wing):

```text
	The museum's new wing has step-free access and touchscreen guides in six languages.
```

## Rule 23: Do not use AI metaphors

AI models use certain words metaphorically to add false gravitas. The literal use of each word is fine.

### Clause A: Do not use AI metaphors

Do not use any of these words as a metaphor:

| Word | Avoid (metaphorical) | Acceptable (literal) |
|------|----------------------|----------------------|
| tapestry | "a tapestry of regulations" | "medieval tapestry" |
| symphony | "a symphony of features" | "Beethoven's symphony" |
| beacon | "a beacon of hope" | "lighthouse beacon" |
| realm | "in the realm of cybersecurity" | an actual kingdom or territory |
| testament | "a testament to innovation" | "last will and testament" |
| ecosystem | "the repair ecosystem" | "Apple's software ecosystem" |
| landscape | "the regulatory landscape" | "Arizona landscape" |
| navigate | "navigate the regulatory process" | "navigate the website" |

**Example 1 - Bad** (The word "tapestry" is used as a metaphor and names nothing the festival holds):

```text
	The festival is a tapestry of local traditions.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence names three events the festival holds):

```text
	The festival includes a parade, a bread-baking contest, and folk dancing.
```

## Rule 24: Do not use academic AI tells

### Clause A: Do not use academic AI tells

Do not use academic AI tells such as "shed light on", "a myriad of", or "prior to". Use the plain equivalent of each phrase.

| Avoid | Use Instead |
|-------|-------------|
| shed light on | clarify, explain, reveal |
| pave the way for | enable, allow, make possible |
| a myriad of | many, numerous, various |
| a plethora of | many, numerous, several |
| paramount | most important, essential, critical |
| pertaining to | about, regarding, concerning |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in light of | because of, given, considering |
| with respect to | about, regarding, for |
| in terms of | regarding, for, about |
| the fact that | that, or rewrite the sentence |

**Example 1 - Bad** (The phrases "Prior to" and "a myriad of" are academic AI tells):

```text
	Prior to the update, a myriad of users reported crashes.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The plain words "Before" and "many" replace them):

```text
	Before the update, many users reported crashes.
```

## Rule 25: Delete hallucinated markup artifacts

AI sometimes reproduces citation markup from its training data, and these strings are certain signs of unedited AI output.

### Clause A: Delete hallucinated markup artifacts

Delete every occurrence of these strings, and put a real reference in its place when the citation was meaningful:

| Artifact | Origin |
|----------|--------|
| `oaicite` | OpenAI ChatGPT citation placeholder |
| `contentReference` | OpenAI internal reference tag |
| `grok_card` | xAI Grok citation tag |
| `attributableIndex` | AI attribution tracking artifact |
| `turn0search0` | ChatGPT search result placeholder |

**Example 1 - Bad** (A ChatGPT citation placeholder is left at the end of the sentence):

```text
	The Sydney Harbour Bridge opened in 1932 :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The placeholder is deleted):

```text
	The Sydney Harbour Bridge opened in 1932.
```

## Rule 26: Quote sources accurately, and set off the long ones

Quote a source word for word, and format the quotation so the source's voice is visually distinct from yours.

### Clause A: Match the source exactly

When you put text in quotation marks and attribute it to a source, make every word match the source exactly. Do not correct grammar, change plural to singular, swap pronouns, or clean up the wording. If you must alter a quote for clarity, mark the change in some way. If the wording is awkward, paraphrase without quotation marks.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The text inside the quotation marks modernizes Lincoln's wording, so it no longer matches the source):

```text
	Lincoln opened the Gettysburg Address with "Eighty-seven years ago our fathers brought forth a new nation."
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The quoted words match the source exactly):

```text
	Lincoln opened the Gettysburg Address with "Four score and seven years ago".
```

### Clause B: Introduce the quote and set off a long one

Name the speaker and the medium when you introduce a quote. Keep short quotes run-in inside the sentence. Set off a long quotation, of more than about fifteen words, as its own indented block introduced by a one-sentence attribution clause.

**Example 1 - Bad** (A quotation of thirty words runs inside the sentence, and neither the speaker nor the medium is named):

```text
	"Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" is a single sentence of thirty words.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (One sentence names the speaker and the speech, and the long quotation is set off as its own block):

```text
	Abraham Lincoln opened his Gettysburg Address in 1863 with this sentence.

	> Four score and seven years ago our fathers brought forth on this continent, a new nation, conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal.
```

## Rule 27: Do not narrate the research process

### Clause A: Do not narrate the research process

Report the facts you can support and silently omit what you cannot. Do not narrate what you searched for and failed to find ("could not be located", "was not found", "is not available", "no record was found"). Do not attach an "as of [date]" qualifier to your own inability to find something. Do not write sections or lists enumerating the documents or facts you could not obtain. Do not add meta-commentary about how the text was put together. If a fact cannot be supported, delete it, and do not tell the reader you looked.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence tells the reader about a search that found nothing):

```text
	No record of the park's opening date could be located as of this writing.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence reports the facts that are supported and leaves the missing one out):

```text
	The park covers two acres and has free entry.
```

## Rule 28: Name the root cause of a difference

When you contrast two things, name the concrete difference that separates them, such as the ingredient, the version, the date, the mechanism, or the material. Do not assert that one is exempt, newer, better, or unaffected without saying what specifically makes it so. If you do not have that detail, do not imply the difference exists.

### Clause A: Name the root cause of a difference

**Example 1 - Bad** (The sentence says cast iron is unaffected and does not say what makes it so):

```text
	Cast iron pans are unaffected by this problem and work fine on induction stoves.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentences name the material property that separates the pans):

```text
	Cast iron works on induction stoves because the material is ferromagnetic, which the cooktop's coil needs to generate heat. Plain aluminum and copper pans are not, so they stay cold unless they have a bonded steel base layer.
```

## Rule 29: Prefer tab indentation

### Clause A: Prefer tab indentation

Prefer tab characters over spaces for indentation. Apply this to code, normal text, and markdown alike.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The nested item is indented with four spaces):

```text
	- Fruit
	    - Apples
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The nested item is indented with one tab character):

```text
	- Fruit
		- Apples
```

## Rule 30: Use bold as a label, not as mid-sentence emphasis

### Clause A: Use bold as a label, not as mid-sentence emphasis

Use bold as a label or prefix at the start of a list item or paragraph, such as `**Local MCP Location**: {text}`, and wherever emphasis genuinely helps. In the middle of a sentence, use it sparingly and reserve it for things that warrant emphasis, since constant mid-sentence bolding becomes noise.

**Example 1 - Bad** (Four words are bolded in the middle of one sentence, so none of them stands out):

```text
	The **backup** runs every **night** and keeps **thirty** days of **snapshots**.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Bold appears once, as a label at the start of the line):

```text
	**Backup schedule**: The backup runs every night and keeps thirty days of snapshots.
```

## Rule 31: Write lists as lists

Write list-shaped content as an actual list, numbered or bulleted, and do not splice it into a sentence with a colon and commas. A colon connecting a label to its value within one item, such as `path: action`, is fine. Apply this in the other direction too by reserving bullets for genuinely list-shaped content and leaving ordinary prose as prose.

### Clause A: Write lists as lists

**Example 1 - Bad** (Four separate settings are spliced into one sentence after a colon):

```text
	This procedure sets a privacy baseline: block third-party cookies, send Do Not Track, disable autofill, block notifications.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Each setting is its own list item):

```text
	This procedure sets a privacy baseline with four settings:

	- Block third-party cookies
	- Send Do Not Track
	- Disable autofill
	- Block notifications
```

## Rule 32: Prefer not to use punctuation as clause connectors

Avoid using a colon, semicolon, or dash to connect two related clauses. Leaning on them as the default way to join thoughts reads as clipped, AI-sounding text. Occasional use is acceptable, but prefer natural connectors wired into the sentence, such as "so", "since", "because", and "which". The exception is highly technical writing, where such punctuation is often the clearest choice.

### Clause A: Prefer not to use punctuation as clause connectors

**Example 1 - Bad** (A colon joins the statement to the steps that follow it):

```text
	The catalog needed organizing: separate the generic bins from the custom ones, then measure everything.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The word "by" joins the two parts inside the sentence):

```text
	The catalog needed organizing by separating the generic bins from the custom ones and then measuring everything.
```

## Rule 33: Put retrospective observations only in retrospective contexts

A retrospective observation is a statement about how something unfolded or developed. Place one only where the purpose of the section, document, or note is itself retrospective.

Look for them inside ordinary sentences. They tend to hide as a short hedge phrase ("in practice", "in theory", "broadly") and not as a clear status statement, and a hedge that reports how something actually went is still a retrospective observation.

### Clause A: Put retrospective observations only in retrospective contexts

**Example 1 - Bad** (A document that defines steps also reports how the steps came about):

```text
	The procedure runs in four steps, in the order below. The steps were written in March and revised in June.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The sentence defines the steps and leaves their history out):

```text
	The procedure runs in four steps, in the order below.
```

## Rule 34: Get to the point

### Clause A: Get to the point

Lead with the answer. Cut verbosity and pointless detail.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The reader asked whether the backup finished, and the answer arrives only at the end of the second sentence):

```text
	I looked into the backup, and there were a few things to check first, including the logs and the disk space. After going through all of that, it turns out the backup finished.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The answer is the first thing the reader sees):

```text
	The backup finished at 23:00.
```

## Rule 35: Write complete sentences with proper grammar

### Clause A: Write complete sentences with proper grammar

Write in complete sentences with proper grammar. Do not clip sentences into fragments to save words.

**Example 1 - Bad** (Both statements are clipped fragments, and nothing says how they relate):

```text
	Backup done. Sync still running.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (One complete sentence carries both facts and how they relate):

```text
	The backup is done, but the sync is still running.
```

## Rule 36: Use proper Markdown formatting

### Clause A: Use proper Markdown formatting

Write list-shaped content as a Markdown list, and use tables, headings, and code blocks when the content calls for them. Do not force everything into paragraphs because the sentences are complete.

**Example 1 - Bad** (Three steps the reader has to follow in order are packed into one paragraph):

```text
	To restore the file, first open Windows Security, after that go to Protection history, and finally choose Restore.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The three steps are a numbered list):

```text
	To restore the file, follow these steps:

	1. Open Windows Security.
	2. Go to Protection history.
	3. Choose Restore.
```

## Rule 37: Use plain, simple English

### Clause A: Use plain, simple English

Use plain, simple English. Do not use jargon, made-up terminology, or metaphors.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The words "socialize", "delta", and "operationalize" are jargon the reader has to translate):

```text
	We need to socialize the delta before we operationalize the fix.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Plain words say what will happen):

```text
	We need to tell the team what changed before we apply the fix.
```

## Rule 38: Leave out what was not asked for

### Clause A: Leave out what was not asked for

Leave out pointless caveats and extra detail that the reader did not ask for.

**Example 1 - Bad** (The reader asked only when the store closes, and the answer adds three things they did not ask for):

```text
	The store closes at 21:00. It opens at 08:00, parking is free, and weekends are busier.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (The answer covers what was asked and stops):

```text
	The store closes at 21:00.
```

## Rule 39: Make every reference readable from the current message

Name a thing in plain language whenever the reader may not know what its ID, item number, acronym, or short name means without scrolling back. Add the ID or number after the name only if it helps.

A reference needs this when it was introduced far back in the conversation, in a tool result or report the reader may not have read, by a different agent, or in a tracker's own numbering such as "item 13". Ask whether the reader would know what it is from this message alone or from the last few exchanges they read, and carry the context if they would not. This is not padding, because a terse line the reader cannot decode costs more than the clause that explains it.

### Clause A: Make every reference readable from the current message

**Example 1 - Bad** (The item numbers mean nothing to a reader who does not have the tracker open):

```text
	Next: do #5 and #4 as one pull request, then #9.
```

**Example 1 - Good** (Each item is named in plain language, with its item number after it):

```text
	Next: raise the inventory consumer's concurrency (item 5, the queue-age alarm that fires again tomorrow) and loosen the first-day ingestion limit (item 4) in one pull request, then unblock the deploy pipeline that has been stuck since November 1 (item 9).
```
